Last time I had a nine qubit circuit that could forecast one patch of atmosphere. The
obvious next question: does that survive contact with a real weather model?

So I rebuilt one. ECMWF's AIFS works in three parts. An **encoder** compresses the weather
grid onto a smaller mesh. A **processor** steps that mesh forward in time. A **decoder**
writes tomorrow's weather back out.

I kept the encoder and decoder classical, and swapped only the processor.

<QWHarness />

That is the whole design. Everything except the middle box is identical across every run,
so any difference in skill belongs to the processor. Real data: ERA5 over Europe at 0.25
degrees, five pressure levels, temperature, wind, pressure and humidity. **1.2 million
numbers in, 1.2 million out.**

The quantum version lost. The rest of this is why I believe that.

## Does the test even work?

The trap in this field is running a quantum model against a weak baseline and declaring
victory. The opposite trap, which is the one I was in danger of, is running it in a slot
where nothing would have worked and declaring defeat.

So before comparing anything to quantum, the setup has to prove it can detect a processor
working at all. Three versions, three random seeds each:

| What is in the slot | Skill |
| --- | --- |
| AIFS style processor | **+0.533** |
| Frozen random block | +0.459 |
| Nothing at all | +0.444 |

A real processor beats an empty slot by **+0.089**. That is a large, stable gap. The test
works, so a quantum null measured on it means something.

Two rows there are unusual and both matter.

**"Nothing at all"** is the floor. Encoder wired straight to decoder. Most benchmarks skip
this, and without it you cannot tell whether your clever block helped or whether the network
around it did the work.

**"Frozen random block"** is a randomly wired processor that is never trained. It still beats
the floor. So if a trained model cannot beat *that*, the training is not doing anything.

<details class="fold">
<summary>Why the frozen random control is the one to steal</summary>

At smaller scales this arm won outright. On a 45 node mesh the frozen random block beat
every trained processor, and the quantum arm matched the empty slot to four decimal places.

That is small sample overfitting: a trained block has parameters to misuse, a frozen one
does not. Training on 319, 639, 1,278 and 2,556 samples, the trained processor's deficit
against random closes steadily from -0.047 to -0.006, and with 10,224 samples it crosses
into a win.

The general lesson: at the scale most quantum machine learning papers work at, a frozen
random baseline may beat your model, and almost nobody runs it.
</details>

## How nine qubits cover a million numbers

A fair question at this point is how a nine qubit circuit touches a mesh of thousands of
nodes. It does not. The same small circuit is slid across the mesh in overlapping patches,
and the overlaps are averaged.

<QWSweep />

This is also why the quantum version is slow. The mesh does not need more qubits, it needs
more *runs*, and the number of runs grows with the mesh.

## The result

In a slot where a classical processor clearly earns its place, the quantum one does not.
Four mesh sizes, three resolutions, four forecast lead times. It never won.

The cleanest comparison came from a control I ran for a different reason. Same data, all
versions on a matched learning rate:

| What is in the slot | Skill | Training time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Frozen random | +0.5556 | 185 s |
| AIFS style | +0.5547 | 198 s |
| Quantum | +0.5522 | **3,777 s** |
| Nothing at all | +0.5517 | 177 s |

Those four numbers span 0.004. They are one number. The quantum processor spent **21 times
the compute** to land exactly where an empty slot lands.

## Four things I had to take back

These are the method, not an apology attached to it. Each one was a single seed telling a
flattering story.

**"The encoding fix is worth ten times the noise."** I had initialised the circuit's angles
about 7.5 times too small, leaving it nearly linear at the start of training. A real
handicap, and mine. Fixing it looked worth +0.014. But I had measured the noise on a
*different* configuration. Six seeds of each gave **+0.019, plus or minus 0.007**. Real
enough to mention, not enough to lean on, and it changes no ranking.

**"More data will close the gap."** The quantum arm does improve faster per doubling of data
than any classical arm. But it starts 0.118 behind and closes at 0.018 a doubling, so it
needs about another seven doublings. There is not that much ERA5. Across the 8x range I
actually ran, the gap went 0.182 to 0.118 and did not close.

**"Sample the record more finely, then."** No. Six hourly instead of daily buys skill at less
than half the rate, because consecutive six hourly states are near duplicates. Samples are
not information.

**"Quantum tops this table."** Briefly, by 0.002. Then I noticed that arm had been handed a
better learning rate than everything it was being compared with. Matching it dissolved the
result. It flattered my own model, which is exactly why I checked.

<details class="fold">
<summary>The one that nearly got away</summary>

The quantum arm collapsed to zero skill twice, once on a seed and once at a data scale, with
the training loss flat for all thirty epochs. No classical arm collapsed once, all project.

I nearly wrote that up as fundamental instability. Then I lowered the learning rate and both
cases trained fine. The honest claim is much narrower: this model needed careful tuning to
train at all, where the classical blocks did not.
</details>

## The biggest mistake was the one I inherited

I took AIFS's architecture and assumed the processor slot was substrate-neutral. It is
not, and the clearest example is not about quantum at all.

AIFS is a **global** model. Nothing enters from outside, because there is no outside. I
transplanted that onto a **regional** box over Europe and gave it none of the machinery a
regional model actually runs on. Real limited-area models are *driven at their edges* by a
coarser global forecast: they are told what is arriving.

Mine was not. So I tested it at longer lead times and got a result that looked like a
finding: the processor's advantage did not just shrink with lead, it **inverted**.

| lead | anemoi minus no-processor |
| --- | --- |
| +1 day | +0.089 |
| +3 days | -0.023 |
| +5 days | -0.035 |
| +7 days | -0.044 |

I nearly wrote that up as a property of processors. It is a property of my domain. At +5
days the weather over Europe depends on what was over the Atlantic, and the model cannot
see it. No processor can compute information that is not in the input, but a processor
with 28,000 parameters can certainly overfit trying.

So I fed the model its boundary: the true state on a ring of cells around the edge, which
is what a driving model provides. The ring is exactly the border already excluded from
scoring, so nothing leaks into the marked region.

| arm | no boundaries | with boundaries |
| --- | --- | --- |
| no processor | +0.345 | +0.486 |
| frozen random | +0.322 | +0.520 |
| **AIFS-style** | +0.310 | **+0.728** |
| **processor advantage** | **-0.034** | **+0.241** |

The inversion was mine. Given the information a regional model normally has, the processor
does not become a liability at long lead. It becomes **nearly three times more valuable**
than at one day.

Two details make this more than a fix. The size of the gain is ordered by how far each arm
can move information: the attention block gained +0.417, the local random one +0.198, and
no-processor +0.142. And the gain grows with lead, exactly as an inflow explanation
requires. That is the mechanism, not just the outcome.

It is also, so far, the only headline in this project that survived being repeated across
three random seeds unchanged.

<details class="fold">
<summary>What this does not show</summary>

Feeding the *true* future boundary is perfect boundary conditions. A real driving model
carries its own error, so this is an upper bound: it isolates the mechanism rather than
simulating an operational forecast.

And boundaries fix the *task*, not the *slot*. At 1 degree the same treatment lifts every
arm from about 0.34 to about 0.85 skill, yet the processor advantage there is only +0.017.
Boundaries decide how much signal exists; mesh size decides whether a processor can use it.
</details>

## It was not losing because it was quantum

Here is the flaw that took longest to see, and it undermines every table above.

The AIFS-style processor is **attention**: it looks at the whole mesh at once. The quantum
processor is a small circuit **slid over the mesh in patches**, moving information one
patch at a time. Those are different kinds of operator. So when the quantum arm lost, two
explanations were tangled together: it was quantum, and it was local. Nothing I had built
could tell them apart.

So I built the missing arm. Same encoder, same decoder, same patch sweep, same receptive
field, same blending, same seed, same parameter count. The **only** difference is whether
the little core inside is a nine-qubit circuit or an ordinary classical map.

| what is in the slot | skill | share of what attention gains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AIFS-style, whole mesh at once | +0.857 | 100% |
| **classical, same patches as the circuit** | **+0.854** | **87%** |
| frozen random, same patches | +0.854 | 82% |
| **the quantum circuit** | **+0.848** | **49%** |
| nothing at all | +0.840 |  -  |

**Most of the gap was never about quantum.** A perfectly ordinary classical operator, given
the same small window the circuit gets, throws away most of the advantage too. On the finer
mesh it is starker: the matched classical arm captures 12% of what attention does, and does
not even beat the frozen random one.

The circuit does lose a bit more on top, and it takes about twenty times the compute to do
it. But the headline finding of this project turns out to be about **architecture, not
substrate**. I spent months measuring a processor slot and most of what I measured was the
shape of the operator, not what it was made of.

<details class="fold">
<summary>The obvious fix, and why it makes things worse</summary>

If the problem is that the patch is too small, spread it out. The nine qubits can sample a
spaced grid instead of a contiguous one, covering a 9x9 area for exactly the same cost.

    3x3 patch    +0.517
    5x5 spread   +0.481
    9x9 spread   +0.447

Monotonically worse. At one day ahead the weather is moved by local advection, so the
neighbouring cells are precisely the ones that matter, and skipping them to reach further
throws away the signal. The circuit's small footprint is *appropriate*, not a handicap.

Which leaves depth or multi-scale as the real fix, and both mean running the circuit many
more times, which is the one thing it cannot afford.
</details>

## What the processor is actually for

One result came out clean. Splitting the classical processor's advantage by variable:

| Variable | Gain from the processor |
| --- | --- |
| Pressure and geopotential | **+0.132** |
| Temperature | +0.069 |
| Wind | +0.066 |
| Humidity | **+0.042** |

Biggest gain on the pressure field, smallest on humidity. That is exactly right. The
processor's job is to move information across the map, so it earns its keep on the large
scale pressure pattern, which is driven by distant conditions, and earns least on humidity,
which is patchy and local.

That is not a quantum result. It is evidence the test measures real atmospheric behaviour,
which is what makes the rest of it worth trusting.

## Scale, and the wall

Because the whole question is whether this follows AIFS upward, I ran the classical version
on a mesh of **41,869 nodes**, the size of AIFS's own. It trained in **85 minutes** on a
laptop GPU.

The quantum processor on that same mesh, costed from its own measured speed, would need
roughly **1,800 hours**. That is the wall, measured rather than argued.

## On a real quantum computer

Everything above is simulated. The architecture claim I keep making is *train classically,
run the forecast on quantum hardware*, so I ran it: encoder and decoder on the laptop, the
processor on IBM's 156 qubit machine, both scored against real ERA5.

First attempt kept **44 per cent** of its simulated skill. Then I looked at why.

<QWTopology />

<QWWireLab />

The circuit connects qubits in a 3x3 grid, matching the map cells, which is what makes it
readable. IBM's chips give each qubit at most three connections. A grid centre needs four.
It cannot be laid down, as a matter of geometry, so the compiler pads it with extra
operations and the error piles up.

Reordering the same nine cells as a snake keeps every connection a genuine neighbour, but
forms a line rather than a grid, and a line fits the chip exactly.

| Circuit | Gates on hardware | Simulated | **On hardware** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3x3 grid | 198 | +0.600 | +0.265 |
| Snake | **40** | +0.598 | **+0.502** |

Same skill in simulation. Double on hardware. **158 of those 198 operations were shuffling,
not computing.**

One more free gain. The part that turns the circuit's measurements into a forecast is a
small classical layer, and it had been trained on perfect simulated measurements while being
fed noisy real ones. Retraining just that layer on the hardware's own output, which needs no
quantum computer at all, reached **96 per cent** of simulated skill.

<details class="fold">
<summary>The control that makes 96 per cent trustworthy, and the cost</summary>

Retraining on twelve cases and scoring on twelve unseen ones could just be fitting the test
set. So: the same retraining on *simulated* output gains +0.007, and a simulator trained
layer applied to hardware is slightly worse than the original. Only the hardware's own
output helps. That isolates it as genuine noise adaptation.

Whole hardware campaign: 2,499 seconds of quantum compute.
</details>

I first wrote this up as a deep tension, that the thing making the circuit interpretable is
the thing hardware cannot do. **That was wrong.** A differently shaped but equally readable
circuit runs natively. It was an unforced error: I designed for the maths and never asked
what the chip could do.

The general point is worth keeping. With a GPU, hardware is interchangeable and only speed
changes. **With a quantum computer the wiring diagram is part of your model.** Identical
weights ran at 44 or 84 per cent fidelity depending only on whether the circuit's shape
matched the chip's.

## What this does and does not show

It shows quantum inference is **not blocked by noise** at this scale. Most of the loss was
a compiler problem.

It does not rescue the model. A 96 per cent faithful hardware forecast is a faithful copy of
something that adds nothing over an empty slot. And the circuit survives hardware *because*
it is small and shallow, which is the same reason it is not useful.

Scope, plainly: this is AIFS *shaped*, not AIFS *scale*. Regional Europe, five levels, one
laptop, against a global model with hundreds of millions of parameters on a cluster. That
does not affect the comparison between processors, since every version shares the same
encoder, decoder and seed. But read it as "does the quantum block do the processor's job",
not as competitive forecast scores.

The bet, that this pays off at scales classical computers cannot reach, is still a bet. I
would rather say that than dress up a null.

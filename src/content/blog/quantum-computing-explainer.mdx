Then a single line placing this as the front door to the series "Quantum Processes in Weather and Climate", and that the maths and simulations here are built and checked, while the framing is yours._

This page is built around a single commitment, and it is worth stating plainly before anything moves. A quantum computer does not try every answer at once. It carries a complex number, an amplitude, for each possible outcome, and it computes by making those amplitudes add and cancel. Useful quantum algorithms are arranged interference, not parallel search. Everywhere below, the amplitudes stay on screen, with their sign and phase, until the moment a measurement squares them into probabilities. If you hold on to that one idea, the rest is geometry.

## 1. A qubit is two angles, not a biased coin

**[PROSE: Alfie]** _Bridge. Brief: set up the Bloch sphere as the honest picture of a single qubit, and flag that the second angle is the part most explanations quietly drop._

A single qubit in a definite (pure) state is a point on the surface of a sphere. Two real angles fix that point: the polar angle $\theta$, measured from the north pole, and the azimuth $\phi$, the longitude. Written in the computational basis,

$$
\ket{\psi} = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\ket{0} + e^{i\phi}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\ket{1}.
$$

The poles are the classical bits: $\theta = 0$ is $\ket{0}$, $\theta = \pi$ is $\ket{1}$. Everywhere else is a genuine superposition. The Cartesian coordinates of the point are not decoration; they are exactly the quantities you can measure, the expectation values of the three Pauli observables:

$$
(\,\langle X\rangle,\ \langle Y\rangle,\ \langle Z\rangle\,) = (\,\sin\theta\cos\phi,\ \sin\theta\sin\phi,\ \cos\theta\,).
$$

Why two angles and not one? A normalised state in $\mathbb{C}^2$ starts with four real numbers. Normalisation removes one. An overall phase factor $e^{i\gamma}$ multiplying the whole state changes nothing you can ever measure, so it removes another. Two real numbers remain: $\theta$ and $\phi$. The distinction that does the work later is this. The **global** phase $\gamma$ is a gauge freedom, invisible by construction. The **relative** phase $\phi$, the longitude, is physical. It is the second degree of freedom, and it is where interference lives.

**Interactive (added in a later build step).** A drag-to-rotate Bloch sphere. You move $\theta$ and $\phi$ independently and watch the state, its amplitudes, and the three expectation values update together. The point of the toy is to make $\phi$ feel like a real axis you can travel along, not a number attached to the side of a formula.

**Common traps.**

- *"A qubit is 0 and 1 at the same time."* It is a definite point on the sphere that simply is not at a pole. "Simultaneously 0 and 1" is word salad; prefer "a specific superposition, with specific amplitudes".
- *"Superposition is an intrinsic property."* It is relative to a basis. A point on the equator is a superposition in the $Z$ basis and a definite state in the $X$ basis. Always ask: superposition of what?
- *"Global and relative phase are the same kind of thing."* Global phase is unobservable and leaves the Bloch point fixed. Relative phase is the longitude and drives everything downstream.
- *"Two Bloch spheres can show two qubits."* They cannot, and the reason is the subject of section 4. Hold the thought.

Foundations for this section: Nielsen and Chuang, chapters 1 and 4; the [Bloch sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloch_sphere) article; [The Qubit Guide](https://qubit.guide); and, for the no-parallelism stance, Aaronson's [Why is quantum computing so hard to explain?](https://www.quantamagazine.org/why-is-quantum-computing-so-hard-to-explain-20210608/)

## 2. Flatten it on purpose

**[PROSE: Alfie]** _Bridge. Brief: name the simplification honestly. We are about to switch off a dimension to build fluency, and we will switch it back on in section 3._

The full sphere is the truth, but it is a lot to hold at once. So we take a deliberate, temporary simplification: set $\phi = 0$ and live on the real great circle through $\ket{0}$, $\ket{+}$, $\ket{1}$, and $\ket{-}$, where $\ket{\pm} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0} \pm \ket{1})$. On this slice the state is a real combination,

$$
\ket{\psi} = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\ket{0} + \sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\ket{1},
$$

and the only rotation that keeps you on the slice is $R_Y(\theta)$. Everything is now angles on a circle, with no imaginary parts to track, and the measurement statistics are just $\cos^2(\theta/2)$ and $\sin^2(\theta/2)$. This is a loan against section 1, and section 3 repays it by switching the phase back on.

**Interactive (added in a later build step).** As you scroll through this section, the three dimensional sphere collapses onto its $X$-$Z$ slice: a dimension is visibly thrown away. Then a slider sweeps $\theta$ while a many-shots histogram shows the measured outcomes tracking $\cos^2(\theta/2)$ and $\sin^2(\theta/2)$. The collapse is scroll-linked because the act of flattening is the lesson; with reduced motion it becomes a button you press.

**Common traps.**

- *"This flat picture is the whole story."* It is a convenience with one axis switched off. Say so out loud, or the reader will quietly assume phase never mattered.
- *"Amplitudes are probabilities."* Even here, keep the amplitudes visible before squaring. An amplitude can be negative, and that negative sign is what makes the cancellation in the next two sections possible.

## 3. Rotations, and the gate you cannot see

**[PROSE: Alfie]** _Bridge. Brief: this is the hinge of the whole piece. Phase comes back, and we make "hidden phase is real" something the reader watches happen rather than takes on trust._

Gates are rotations of the Bloch point. With the standard convention $R_P(t) = e^{-i t P / 2}$ for a Pauli $P$, the three coordinate rotations are

$$
R_X(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\tfrac{t}{2} & -i\sin\tfrac{t}{2} \\[2pt] -i\sin\tfrac{t}{2} & \cos\tfrac{t}{2} \end{pmatrix},\quad
R_Y(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\tfrac{t}{2} & -\sin\tfrac{t}{2} \\[2pt] \sin\tfrac{t}{2} & \cos\tfrac{t}{2} \end{pmatrix},\quad
R_Z(t) = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i t/2} & 0 \\[2pt] 0 & e^{+i t/2} \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Now the lesson the section exists for. The measured value of $Z$ is

$$
\langle Z\rangle = |\alpha|^2 - |\beta|^2 = \cos\theta,
$$

which depends on $\theta$ alone. It does not contain $\phi$. So $R_Z(\lambda)$, which sends $\phi \to \phi + \lambda$, slides the state around a line of latitude and changes $\langle X\rangle$ and $\langle Y\rangle$, while leaving $\langle Z\rangle$ and the entire $Z$-basis outcome distribution untouched. In the computational basis, $R_Z$ is invisible.

The phase is not gone; it is waiting for a change of basis. A Hadamard is exactly the rotation between the $Z$ basis and the $X$ basis, since $H Z H = X$. Apply $R_Z(\phi)$ to $\ket{+}$ and then $H$, and the $Z$-readout sweeps deterministically from $\ket{0}$ to $\ket{1}$ as $\phi$ runs from $0$ to $\pi$:

| $\phi$ | $Z$ outcome before $H$ | $Z$ outcome after $H$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | 50 / 50 | always 0 |
| $\pi/2$ | 50 / 50 | 50 / 50 |
| $\pi$ | 50 / 50 | always 1 |

The middle column never moves: phase is invisible to $Z$. The right column is a clean sweep: phase is fully visible once you change basis. That sweep is interference, the two amplitudes made to add in phase or in anti-phase at the output.

**Interactive (added in a later build step).** A single-qubit simulator. Build a short sequence of $R_X$, $R_Y$, $R_Z$ gates; watch the Bloch point move and the $\langle X\rangle$, $\langle Y\rangle$, $\langle Z\rangle$ bars respond. A basis toggle lets you confirm by hand that $R_Z$ does nothing visible until a Hadamard turns the hidden phase into a measurable swing.

**Common traps.**

- *"$R_Z$ does nothing."* It does plenty. It does nothing you can see in the $Z$ basis, which is the whole point: the phase is real and hidden.
- *"Phase is cosmetic."* $\ket{+}$ and $\ket{-}$ have identical $Z$ statistics and opposite futures under the next gate. Make the reader watch the difference appear.
- *"Randomness enters at the gates."* Gates are deterministic, reversible rotations. Randomness enters only at measurement. Keep the two separate.
- *"One measurement reveals the state."* It does not. Show a histogram over many shots; a single shot is almost always misleading.

Conventions and the basis-change picture: Nielsen and Chuang chapter 4; the [Qiskit gate reference](https://docs.quantum.ibm.com/api/qiskit/qiskit.circuit.library.RZGate) for the exact sign and half-angle; IBM Quantum Learning for the teaching framing.

## 4. Two qubits, and a correlation no pair of spheres can hold

**[PROSE: Alfie]** _Bridge. Brief: entanglement is the natural next rung, and it settles the debt from section 1. Say clearly that it is important machinery for the general story, and that the payoff in section 5 will deliberately not need it._

Add a second qubit and a controlled-NOT. Starting from $\ket{+}\ket{0}$ and applying CNOT gives the Bell state

$$
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(\ket{00} + \ket{11}\big),
$$

whose two qubits are correlated in a way that no pair of separate Bloch points can represent. Each qubit on its own, looked at in isolation, sits at the centre of its sphere, maximally uncertain. That is the promise from section 1 settled: a Bloch sphere describes one qubit, and a genuinely entangled pair has no faithful two-sphere picture. The information is in the joint state, not in the parts.

The honest way to see entanglement is statistical. Measure one qubit, note the outcome, and look at where the other one lands: a single point for a product state, a line for a classical correlation, a spread-out cloud for an entangled one. It is a correlation in the joint amplitude table, nothing more occult than that.

One caution belongs here, not later. More entanglement is not automatically more computational power. In careful machine-learning benchmarks, removing entanglement often leaves performance as good or better, which is exactly why the payoff in the next section is built to stand on a single qubit.

**Interactive (added in a later build step).** Add or remove qubits, place a CNOT, measure one wire, and watch the conditional distribution of the other as a point cloud plus a many-shots histogram. The circuit stays legible as it grows, and the qubit count stays small, because the state vector doubles in size with every wire.

**Common traps.**

- *"Two Bloch spheres show a two-qubit state."* Not an entangled one: each half of a Bell state is maximally mixed and sits at the centre, so all maximally entangled states would look identical.
- *"Entanglement is spooky action or parallel universes."* Stay at the level of mechanism: correlations in the joint amplitudes.
- *"More entanglement means more power."* Not as a rule, and not at these scales. Treat it as machinery, not magic.
- *"These toys scale."* They do not. A state vector is exponential in the number of qubits; this is a pedagogical model with a handful of wires.

The conditional point-cloud technique follows Gidney's [Visualizing 2-qubit entanglement](https://algassert.com/post/1716); the benchmark caution follows Bowles, Ahmed and Schuld, *Better than classical? The subtle art of benchmarking quantum machine learning models* (2024).

## 5. The payoff: one qubit as a tunable Fourier series

**[PROSE: Alfie]** _Bridge. Brief: this is the destination and the on-ramp to your research. It pays back the phase axis from section 1 and the hidden-phase lesson from section 3, and it does so entirely on one qubit. Keep the tone earned, not triumphant._

Here is the move that connects all of the above to machine learning. Take one qubit. Encode a single real input $x$ by a rotation whose generator is the Pauli $Z$, $S(x) = e^{-i x Z / 2}$, and interleave $L$ copies of that encoding with trainable single-qubit rotations $W$. This repetition of the input is called data re-uploading. Measure $\langle Z\rangle$ at the end and call the result $f(x)$. The claim is that this circuit is a Fourier series in $x$:

$$
f(x) = \sum_{n=-L}^{L} c_n\, e^{i n x}, \qquad c_{-n} = c_n^{*}.
$$

Three things make this the whole story in miniature. First, the accessible frequencies are the integers $\{0, 1, \dots, L\}$. They come from differences of sums of the encoding generator's eigenvalues, which for $Z/2$ are $\pm\tfrac{1}{2}$, so the gap is $1$ and that gap is the base frequency. Second, each extra re-uploading layer adds exactly one new top frequency: going from $L$ to $L+1$ widens the spectrum from $\{-L,\dots,L\}$ to $\{-(L+1),\dots,L+1\}$ and nothing else. Third, the encoding sets the frequencies, and the trainable gates set the coefficients $c_n$. Depth buys bandwidth; training shapes what you do with it.

This is the one place where the page checks itself rather than asking you to take a derivation on faith. The toy below computes $f(x)$ from the same simulator the earlier toys use, takes its discrete Fourier transform, and overlays the frequencies the theory predicts. The same circuit is also checked in a small test that runs outside the page, where the measured spectrum is confirmed to sit exactly on the integers $0$ to $L$, the reality condition $c_{-n} = c_n^{*}$ holds, and adding a layer adds precisely one tone. If the picture and the prediction ever disagreed, that would be a bug, and you would see it.

There is a guardrail that has to travel with this result, or the result gets oversold. A single qubit with $L$ layers reaches the full set of frequencies $\{0,\dots,L\}$, but it cannot set the corresponding coefficients freely. Controlling $K+1$ complex coefficients arbitrarily needs at least $2K+1$ real parameters, and the number of ways the circuit can build a high frequency shrinks as the frequency grows, so high-frequency coefficients are structurally small. Full coefficient control, and the universal-approximation story that comes with it, needs width: more qubits, not just more depth. The honest one-line version is that the spectrum overstates the expressivity. That caveat is not a footnote; it is the reason the framing in the next section is defensible.

**Interactive (added in a later build step).** Encode $x$, set the number of re-uploading layers $L$, and see two linked panels: the function $f(x) = \langle Z\rangle$ across the input range, and its measured spectrum with the predicted integer frequencies marked. Increasing $L$ (by slider, and scroll-linked through this section) makes the comb of frequencies widen one tooth at a time. The spectrum you see is the spectrum the test verifies.

**Common traps.**

- *"Re-uploading just feeds the data in again, so nothing changes."* A single encoding gives one frequency, a plain sinusoid. Re-uploading is precisely what widens the spectrum.
- *"One qubit can realise any degree-$L$ Fourier function."* No. It reaches every frequency up to $L$ but cannot choose the coefficients independently. Say "depth buys bandwidth; coefficient control needs width".
- *"A frequency is in the spectrum, so the model can use it."* The spectrum overstates expressivity; some coefficients are forced small or vanish. Pair any Fourier-interpretability claim with this.
- *"This is the convention everyone uses."* With $Z/2$ the gap is $1$ and the frequencies are clean integers. With the bare Pauli $Z$ the gap is $2$ and a single layer gives $\{-2, 0, 2\}$. State the convention or the frequency axis will not match other write-ups.

The result is Schuld, Sweke and Meyer, [The effect of data encoding on the expressive power of variational quantum machine learning models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.08605) (2021); the single-qubit construction is Pérez-Salinas et al., [Data re-uploading for a universal quantum classifier](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.02085) (2020). The coefficient guardrail is Barthe and Pérez-Salinas, [Gradients and frequency profiles of quantum re-uploading models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10822) (2024), and Mhiri et al., [Constrained and vanishing expressivity of quantum Fourier models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09417) (2025). For interactive precedents, PennyLane's [Quantum models as Fourier series](https://pennylane.ai/qml/demos/tutorial_expressivity_fourier_series) is the near-exact template for the toy.

**[PROSE: Alfie]** _On-ramp, in your voice. A closing word on expectations, so the series starts honestly. No published quantum machine-learning result for weather or climate shows a clear advantage over classical methods; the most careful outcomes are parity, or classical models doing better, and added entanglement frequently does not help. There is also a growing body of work showing that the quantum models which are easy to train tend to be the ones a classical method can reproduce. The interest in the work ahead is not a speed claim. It is what a Fourier-decomposable model can tell you about structure in the data, examined carefully and reported without spin.

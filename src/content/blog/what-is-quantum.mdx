## 1. What this is, and what a quantum computer is for

**[PROSE: Alfie — provisional draft, replace in your voice]**

I work in climate and machine learning, and I have been teaching myself quantum computing on the side, partly because it keeps turning up near my research and partly because the headlines made me curious. This is not an expert's explainer. It is me writing down the handful of ideas that finally made it click, in plain language, and pointing you to the people who explain it far better than I can. If you keep seeing "quantum" in headlines and want an honest, jargon-light place to start, this is roughly the page I wish I had found first. I have stuck to what I am fairly sure of and flagged where I am still hazy. And one promise: you will not read "tries every answer at once" here, except to explain why it is the thing almost everyone, me included at first, gets wrong.

So, what is a quantum computer actually for? Not a faster laptop. It is a different machine that should one day be good at a short, specific list of problems, simulating molecules and materials, some kinds of optimisation, and, more speculatively, parts of machine learning, and no better than a pocket calculator at most of what you do all day. The interesting question was never "how fast". It is "good at what".

**The short version, before any detail.** A qubit is a direction you set, not a switch that is somehow both on and off. You cannot read that direction; looking forces a plain 0 or 1 and destroys the rest. The cleverness is arranging things so the wrong answers cancel out and the right one reinforces. And it is early and narrow: for most tasks, including anything in my own field, there is no clear advantage yet. The rest of this page takes those ideas one at a time, with a couple of things you can play with, and ends with the resources that actually helped me. If you then want the real machinery, the [companion piece](/writing/quantum-computing-explainer/) is right next door.

## 2. Why is everyone suddenly talking about it?

**[PROSE: Alfie — provisional draft, replace in your voice]**

Three real things happened at roughly the same time, and each one comes with a caveat worth keeping.

First, a genuine milestone. In late 2024, Google's Willow chip passed a line the field had chased since the 1990s: adding more of the error-prone physical parts made the result cleaner, not messier, which is the opposite of what normally happens and the thing error correction has to do to ever scale. The caveat is that Google itself frames the remaining gap as enormous: from today's roughly one error in a thousand to the one in a trillion a genuinely useful machine would need. A marker on a long road, not the destination.

Second, a steady trend. The reliability of the basic two-qubit operations has climbed over years, from coin-flip unreliable to better than 99.9% in the best labs. No single date attached, just a line going the right way.

Third, a wall of money. Investment in quantum start-ups reached around 12.6 billion dollars in 2025, almost all of it private and about six times the year before, with governments committing billions more through national programmes. The caveat is the important one: a wave of money means a lot of people are betting it will work, not that it works yet.

So the headlines make sense. But the machines today are still small and noisy, the genuinely useful applications are not here yet, and the loudest claims tend to be the ones to distrust.

## 3. What is a qubit?

A normal bit is a switch: it is either 0 or 1. The picture that finally made a qubit click for me is an **arrow you can tilt**. Point it straight up and it means 0. Point it straight down and it means 1. The interesting part is everywhere in between: the arrow can point at any angle, and where you aim it sets the *odds* of getting 0 or 1 when you eventually look. Near the top, you will almost always get 0; near the bottom, almost always 1; pointing sideways, it is roughly a coin toss.

A qubit is a direction you have chosen, and that tilt sets the odds. One more thing about the arrow matters later, so hold the thought: as well as tilting it up or down, you can turn it to face different sideways directions, and that sideways setting changes nothing about the odds when you look once. It seems pointless now. If a setting you can change but never see sounds pointless, that is the right reaction for the moment; we are deliberately leaving it parked. It turns out to be the part that does the real work in section 5.

<QubitArrow />

Tilt the arrow and read off the odds. It is worth pausing on what this picture is *not*. You will often hear that a qubit is "0 and 1 at the same time". That is the wrong words. The arrow is set to one direction, and "both at once" wrongly suggests you could somehow read out both answers, which you cannot: when you look, you get one. It is not secretly storing both answers for later, and a row of qubits is not quietly holding every combination at once. Where the arrow points is real and specific. What you can *do* with it is the subject of the next two sections.

## 4. How do you get an answer out?

Everything so far has been friendly. This next part is the one genuinely hard idea in the post, so it is worth slowing down for. It is the catch that makes quantum computing hard, and the single most counterintuitive thing for a newcomer. You cannot read the arrow's direction. The only thing you are allowed to do is **look**, and looking forces the qubit to answer with a plain 0 or 1, with odds set by the tilt. The moment it answers, the direction is gone. Your instinct here is probably to look again, more carefully, and that is a fair thing to want. But there is nothing left to look at: that one answer is all the qubit will ever tell you about the tilt.

So a single look gives you one answer, 0 or 1, and erases the tilt that was hiding the odds. To see those odds, you have to prepare the same arrow again and again, a fresh, identically tilted arrow each time since the last one is spent, and look at each one.

<QubitArrow measure />

Set a tilt, then look once: you get a single 0 or 1. Look a hundred times and the tally settles close to the odds the arrow was holding, though not exactly, because a hundred looks is still only a sample. The tilt sets the odds; each look spends them on one answer; only many looks reveal them. So we have two facts to carry forward: an arrow whose tilt only sets the odds, and a look that spends those odds on a single answer and destroys the tilt. Hold on to "looking is destructive", because it is exactly why the obvious idea in the next section does not work.

## 5. So how could it ever help?

**[PROSE: Alfie — provisional draft, replace in your voice]**

Now the part I got most wrong at first. The tempting story is that the machine quietly tries every answer at once and hands you the winner. From the last section you can already see the hole in it: if the qubits were just a blur over all possible answers and you looked, you would get one of them at random, no better than a guess, and you could have saved a great deal of money.

The real trick uses the arrow's hidden sideways setting from section 3. A quantum computation can reach the same answer by several different routes at once. Each route carries one of those sideways settings, like the crest or trough of a wave, and crests and troughs can add up or cancel out. You arrange things so the routes to the *wrong* answers arrive out of step and flatten each other to nothing, the way two sound waves can cancel to silence, while the routes to the *right* answer line up and reinforce. Then, when you look, the right answer is overwhelmingly the one you get. The slider below is the whole idea in miniature: two contributions can line up and add, or oppose and vanish.

<TwoWaves />

Cancelling wrong answers is the thing an ordinary computer cannot easily do, and arranging it on purpose is the actual craft of quantum computing. It only pays off for specially structured problems: simulating quantum systems like molecules and materials, some kinds of optimisation, and, more speculatively, parts of machine learning. For most tasks there is no advantage at all, and even where there might be, it is early days.

## The resources that actually helped me

Honestly, the things below taught me far more than I could fit on one page, and pointing you to them is half the reason I wrote this. This is the short shelf that helped most, roughly in the order I would hand them to a friend. The companion piece, [Quantum Computing from First Principles](/writing/quantum-computing-explainer/), is the natural next step if you want the real machinery.

**Watch**

- [Quantum Computing in 5 Levels of Difficulty](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OWJCfOvochA) (WIRED, with Talia Gershon of IBM). A good first five minutes: a real researcher, an honest spinning-coin prop, and the difficulty dialled up level by level.
- [What makes quantum computers SO powerful?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-UrdExQW0cs) (Veritasium). Good for *seeing* the real mechanism, and it centres interference rather than "trying everything at once".
- [But what is quantum computing? (Grover's Algorithm)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RQWpF2Gb-gU) (3Blue1Brown). The honest full picture once you want a little more: it opens by clearing up the misconceptions, then builds the interference idea up visually. A notch more advanced, and worth it.
- [Don't fall for quantum hype](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b-aGIvUomTA) (Sabine Hossenfelder). A reality check if the headlines have been getting to you, without dismissing the science.

**Read**

- [Why Is Quantum Computing So Hard to Explain?](https://www.quantamagazine.org/why-is-quantum-computing-so-hard-to-explain-20210608/) (Scott Aaronson, Quanta). The one to read if you read one: why "0 and 1 at once" misleads, and what is actually going on.
- [What is Superposition?](https://www.quera.com/glossary/superposition) (QuEra). A short, candid definition that refuses the "0 and 1 at once" line.
- [Quantum Computing for the Very Curious](https://quantum.country/qcvc) (Nielsen and Matuschak). A heavier, rewarding read that treats a qubit as an arrow and never resorts to the myth.
- [Basic Quantum Computing: Introduction](https://medium.com/@charlie.thomas_94667/basic-quantum-computing-introduction-f83dcfbd0e40) (Charlie Thomas). A warm, from-scratch series to work through at your own pace, honest that the hard part is the order you learn things in, not your ability.

Most explanations of quantum computing's limits stop at "the hardware is noisy". True, and
the least interesting constraint, because it is the one already getting the money.

I spent months building a quantum weather model. Noise turned out not to be the binding
problem at all. On real IBM hardware it kept
[96 per cent of its simulated skill](/writing/quantum-weather-model-full-scale). It still
did not beat doing nothing, for reasons that had nothing to do with noise.

So here is the other list. Five things that decide whether a quantum computer helps with
your problem, what would have to change for each, and who is actually affected. They are not
a sequence. Any one of them alone is enough to sink you.

## Getting data in

A quantum computer's power lives in a space that grows exponentially with the number of
qubits. The catch is filling it.

If you have a million classical numbers, loading them takes about a million operations. The
exponential space you gained is paid for in linear time, **every time you run the circuit**.
For machine learning that means every sample, every pass, every epoch.

In my weather model this was the whole story. The circuit only ever saw a squeezed summary
of the input and only ever handed back a handful of numbers. Everything quantum happened
between an ordinary map in and an ordinary map out. You cannot tune your way out of a
doorway: I swept the encoding settings and the best available option moved the result by
about 0.019, barely above noise.

**What would fix it:** not loading classical data at all. If your input is *already* a
quantum thing, like a molecule, you skip this entirely. That is not a footnote. It is the
single strongest filter on what is plausible.

<details class="fold">
<summary>The paper that says this is permanent</summary>

[The Input Problem: Permanent Bottleneck for QML](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08433)
(August 2026) argues the encoding cost is structural rather than an engineering phase we
grow out of. I found it after arriving at the same conclusion the slow way.
</details>

## Quantum models are kernel methods

This one should change how you read most quantum machine learning papers.

A quantum model with a fixed way of loading data can be written as a **kernel method**, a
well understood classical technique from the 1990s. That reframing is due to Maria Schuld.

So a kernel baseline belongs in every comparison, and it usually is not there. But do not
take the stronger version of this, which I got wrong at first: kernels do not *replace*
neural networks as the peer class. Schuld's own follow-up work benchmarks quantum models
against support vector machines **and** neural networks, and finds ordinary classical models
win. And the kernel view is not a universal stand-in either, since some quantum models
provably need less data than any kernel method on certain tasks.

**What would fix it:** a way of loading data whose kernel is genuinely hard to compute
classically *and* happens to suit a real problem. Both halves are needed. A hard kernel that
encodes the wrong assumptions is just an expensive way to be wrong.

<details class="fold">
<summary>The citation, and the caveat I nearly missed</summary>

The result is [Schuld (2021), arXiv:2101.11020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.11020), a single
author preprint. I originally cited it as "Schuld et al." in *Phys. Rev. A* 103, 032430,
which is a **different paper** (Schuld, Sweke and Meyer,
[arXiv:2008.08605](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.08605), on encoding and expressive power). I
had merged two papers into one citation, and a fact check caught it before publication.

The caveat that matters: [Bowles, Ahmed and Schuld](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07059)
benchmark twelve quantum models against SVMs, feed-forward networks and CNNs, so the author
of the kernel result keeps neural networks in the comparison. And Jerbi et al.
(*Nat. Commun.* 14, 517, 2023) prove kernel methods can need exponentially more data than
data re-uploading models on some tasks, so "quantum models are just kernels" is too strong.

Worth adding that my circuit was **not** feeble. I tested whether a classical formula using
all single frequencies plus all 351 pairs could reproduce its output, and it managed only
$R^2 = 0.177$. Switch the entangling off and the same formula gets 0.845. So the circuit
does create real structure that simple classical maps cannot copy. It just is not structure
that helps predict weather.

I have now had that same answer four times: on weather patches, on chaotic toy systems, on a
vertical column model, and on a full forecast. Every time, once the right baseline is in the
table, the advantage disappears.
</details>

## Getting answers out

Measuring a quantum state destroys it, and you get very little back. The Holevo bound caps
the information you can extract from nine qubits at nine bits, and that is the cap for the
*best possible* measurement strategy, not the one you happen to run. Any actual measurement
usually recovers far less.

So even a perfect quantum computer holding a gorgeous exponentially large answer has to hand
it over a few bits at a time. If what you want is a **whole field**, like tomorrow's
temperature at every point on a map, you are reading all of it out anyway and the quantum
part was a detour.

<details class="fold">
<summary>Two things people get wrong about this, including me</summary>

The bound is on **accessible information**, the maximum over all measurement strategies, not
"bits per measurement". The gap between a given measurement and that maximum can be
arbitrarily large.

And the cap is not absolute: with pre-shared entanglement, superdense coding sends two
classical bits per transmitted qubit, exactly double.

Most importantly, Holevo limits how much you can **read out**, not how much computation
produced it. That is why exponential quantum advantages in communication complexity and in
learning coexist with it perfectly happily.
</details>

**What would fix it:** problems whose *answer is small*. An energy. A yes or no. One optimal
setting. Quantum chemistry fits perfectly: huge internal state, one number out. Full field
weather forecasting is close to the worst possible fit, which is worth knowing before you
spend a year on it, as I did.

## The chip's wiring is part of your model

This is the most practical of the five, and the one that surprised me.

With a graphics card, hardware is interchangeable. The same code runs anywhere and only the
speed changes. With a quantum computer it is not. Each qubit is physically wired to only a
few neighbours, and if your circuit wants a different shape, the compiler has to shuffle
qubits around. Every shuffle costs accuracy.

<QWWireLab />

My circuit connected qubits in a 3x3 grid to match map cells. IBM's chips allow three
connections per qubit. A grid centre needs four. It simply cannot be laid down. The compiler
turned 60 operations into 198, and the errors followed.

Reordering the same nine cells as a snake keeps every connection a real neighbour but forms
a line, and lines fit. Forty operations, no shuffling, and double the accuracy on hardware
for identical simulated skill.

**What would fix it:** designing the circuit and the chip choice together, or hardware with
richer wiring. Trapped ion machines connect everything to everything and would have run my
original circuit with no penalty at all.

<details class="fold">
<summary>This is a trade, not a mistake, and I should not have called it one</summary>

Building the circuit around the chip's wiring has a name, the
[hardware efficient ansatz](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.05018) (Kandala et al., *Nature* 549,
2017). I first wrote that ignoring it was "a beginner's mistake". A fact check pushed back,
and it was right to.

Qubit routing is a mature compiler job, and a connectivity-agnostic circuit still runs
correctly, just deeper. Deliberately choosing a problem-shaped entangler and paying the
routing cost is expert judgement, not an error: chemistry-motivated circuits do it
routinely, in exchange for better trainability. So the honest framing is that ignoring
topology has a **fidelity price** you should measure, which in my case was more than half my
hardware accuracy, and which I had not measured.

Going the other way is not free either. Hardware-shaped circuits are more prone to
[barren plateaus](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.11173), where the training signal vanishes, and
an earlier paper from 2022
[questions whether they are practically useful at all](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.01477).
</details>

## The simulability trap

The uncomfortable one.

Quantum models suffer from vanishing training signal as they get bigger. So people add
structure to keep them trainable. The trap is that the structure which makes a circuit
trainable tends to be the same structure that makes it **easy to simulate on a normal
computer**.

That leaves a narrow and possibly empty window: circuits interesting enough to matter,
structured enough to train, and still hard to simulate.

There is a sharp practical consequence. If you can simulate your quantum model on a laptop,
as I did, then by definition it is doing something a laptop can do.

**What would fix it:** nobody knows. This is an open research problem, not a roadmap item,
and it is the honest answer to "when will quantum machine learning work".

## So who is actually affected

I first wrote this as a single ranking. A fact check pointed out that my ranking quietly
switched axes halfway down, which is exactly the move I accuse optimisation vendors of. So
here are two rankings, and the axis is stated first.

**Ranked by what has actually been delivered:**

1. **Sensing, first by a wide margin.** This is the only area with systems in production.
   SQUID magnetometers, atomic vapour magnetometers and atomic clocks are old news, not
   near-term promises. Squeezed light runs in LIGO's production detectors and now pushes
   quantum noise below the standard quantum limit.
2. **Cryptography**, as a planning problem. Nothing has been broken, but the migration to
   post-quantum encryption is real work happening now.
3. **Chemistry, optimisation and machine learning.** None has demonstrated a practical
   advantage on a real problem.

**Ranked by the quality of the theoretical case, which comes out differently:**

1. **Optimisation and machine learning on classical data** both have proven separations on
   structured problems. That surprised me, given the next section.
2. **Chemistry** has the most careful cost estimates of anything in the field, but no
   evidence of exponential advantage for ground-state energy.

So chemistry does not top either list on its own. What it leads on is **how precisely we
understand what it would cost**, which is a real and underrated thing to lead on.

<details class="fold">
<summary>Where I had chemistry wrong, and the strongest pushback on my optimisation take</summary>

I wrote that chemistry fits because "the input is already quantum". **That is false as
stated.** In every serious resource estimate the input is classical: a nuclear geometry plus
a basis set. The quantum state is an *output* that has to be prepared, at cost. The Feynman
intuition is exactly what the field's own senior figures now decline to endorse. Lee et al.
(*Nat. Commun.* 14, 1952, 2023) conclude that evidence for exponential advantage across
chemical space "has yet to be found".

On optimisation, my "no demonstrated speedup" line is defensible about *deployed* systems
but ignores recent theory. Decoded Quantum Interferometry has a superpolynomial advantage
claim on structured optimisation problems (*Nature* 646, 831, 2025). Someone in that field
would lead with it, and they would be entitled to.

And on machine learning, my own worst-fit category, Liu, Arunachalam and Temme
(*Nature Physics*, 2021) proved a rigorous exponential separation for a learning problem
built on discrete logarithms. It is a constructed problem rather than a natural dataset, but
it means "quantum ML cannot help" is too strong. The correct claim is narrower: on *ordinary
classical data*, of the kind I was using, all five bottlenecks apply at once.
</details>

## The narrower goal

None of this says quantum computing does not work. It says the shape of the problem is not
the shape of the hype.

The hype is that quantum computers are faster computers that will speed up whatever you
already do. The reality is that they are a **different kind of machine suited to a narrow
class of problems**, mostly defined by whether your data is already quantum and whether your
answer is small.

For everyday impact, quantum sensing is already reaching you, in navigation, medical imaging
and timekeeping. Chemistry is the most plausible route to better batteries and catalysts,
but be honest about the distance: the cheapest known algorithm puts an industrial catalyst
like FeMoco at roughly **four million physical qubits and four days of runtime**, which is a
harder hardware target than breaking RSA, against today's devices in the low thousands. Then
add materials qualification and manufacturing on top. I would not put a decade on it, and
anyone who does is guessing.

Your weather forecast stays classical, and my own experiments are a small piece of the
evidence for why.

That is narrower than the marketing. It is also more useful, because it tells you where to
point the effort. The best thing a negative result does is stop someone else spending a year
finding out the same thing.

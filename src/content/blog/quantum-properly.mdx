## 1. What a quantum computer is actually for

A quantum computer is not a faster laptop. It will not make your email load quicker or your spreadsheets recalculate sooner. For very nearly everything you do on a computer all day, a quantum machine would be no better than a pocket calculator, and usually much worse. That is not a flaw. It is the point: it is a different kind of machine, built for a different kind of job.

I work in climate and machine learning, and I use quantum machine learning at the edge of my own research, so I have spent a long while getting to grips with this, including the unglamorous part. In my own field, weather and climate, no published result yet shows a clear advantage over an ordinary computer. I would rather open with that than bury it, because the honest shape of this subject is more interesting than the hype, and this is the no-equations way in to it. There is a companion piece that goes properly into the machinery.

So if it is not a faster laptop, what is it for? A short list. A quantum computer is expected, one day, to be genuinely good at simulating nature itself, the behaviour of molecules and materials that chemists would love to predict without years in a lab. It may help with certain kinds of optimisation, and, more speculatively, with parts of machine learning. Outside that list there is no expected advantage at all. The interesting question was never how fast. It is good at what, and the honest answer is: a few specific things, and we are still early.

<aside class="callout callout-key">

**The whole idea, in four lines**

1. A qubit is a *direction you set*, not a switch that is somehow both on and off.
2. You cannot read that direction. *Looking forces a plain 0 or 1, and destroys the rest.*
3. The trick is to arrange things so the *wrong answers cancel out* and the *right one reinforces*.
4. It is *early and narrow*. For most tasks, including ones in my own field, there is no clear advantage yet.

</aside>

Quantum computing has also picked up a few catchy phrases that are repeated everywhere, and each one quietly points you the wrong way. Here they are, so you can spot them in the wild.

<aside class="callout callout-refuse">

**Three lines we are refusing**

- *"It tries every answer at once."* The most common one, and the one the rest of this is built to correct.
- *"A qubit is 0 and 1 at the same time."* The wrong words for something real. It suggests you could read out both answers, which you cannot.
- *"It uses parallel universes."* A hotly disputed interpretation, not the mechanism, and you do not need it to follow any of this.

</aside>

Each is half-pointing at something genuine, and the satisfying part is seeing exactly where it goes wrong. So the rest of this is not facts to take on trust. It is the small set of ideas that let you catch the dodgy phrasing yourself, and, by the end, say why it is wrong in a sentence.

## 2. Why is everyone suddenly talking about it?

Three real things happened at roughly the same time, and each comes with a caveat the headlines tend to drop. Keep both halves together and the picture stops feeling like hype.

First, a genuine milestone. The parts of these machines are fragile and pick up errors constantly, and the obvious fix, adding more parts to check the work, has historically just added more things to go wrong. The goal since the 1990s has been a turning point where adding more error-prone parts makes the result *cleaner*. In December 2024, Google's Willow chip crossed that line for the first time. The caveat, which Google states plainly, is that the remaining gap is enormous: roughly one error in a thousand today, against the one in a trillion a useful machine would need. A marker on a long road, with no date attached.

<aside class="gloss">Willow "below threshold", December 2024: Google Quantum AI, <em>Nature</em> 638, 920 (2024), doi:10.1038/s41586-024-08449-y.</aside>

Second, a steady trend. The reliability of the basic two-qubit operation has climbed over years, from coin-flip unreliable to better than 99.9% in the best labs. That is a trend line, not a finish line: it tells you the engineering is working, not when it arrives.

<aside class="gloss">Two-qubit gate fidelity climbing roughly 50 to 90 to 99 to 99.9%; the three-nines mark first crossed by Ballance and Lucas (Oxford), Phys. Rev. Lett. (2016).</aside>

Third, a wall of money. Investment in quantum start-ups reached around 12.6 billion dollars in 2025, roughly six times the year before. This is the one to be most careful with. Money measures belief, not capability. Capability is measured in the lab, in errors and fidelities, not on a balance sheet.

<aside class="gloss">About 12.6 billion USD into quantum start-ups in 2025, roughly 6.3 times the prior year: McKinsey Quantum Technology Monitor (2026).</aside>

So the headlines make sense, but put the three together and you land somewhere calmer. We are still in the small, noisy era, and the genuinely useful applications will need far more, and far steadier, qubits than the handful we have. Worth carrying with you: the loudest claims have tended to be the ones to distrust, and several of the biggest of the past couple of years drew serious public pushback from other scientists. That is not cynicism. It is the field correcting itself in public, which is exactly what you want to see.

## 3. What is a qubit?

A normal bit is a switch: 0 or 1, with nothing in between. The picture that finally made a qubit sit still for me is an **arrow you can tilt**.

Point it straight up and it means 0; straight down, 1. The interesting part is everything in between. The arrow can rest at any angle, and where you aim it sets the **odds** of getting 0 or 1 when you eventually look. Near the top, almost always 0; near the bottom, almost always 1; sideways, roughly a coin toss. A qubit is a direction you have chosen, and the tilt sets the odds.

<BlochLab />

Have a play. Notice there is no special "both" position. Every angle is one definite direction with its own odds.

There is one more thing you can do, and I will mention it now and then deliberately leave it alone. As well as tilting up or down, you can swivel the arrow to face different sideways directions. The curious bit: that sideways setting changes nothing about the odds when you look once. So for now it looks pointless, a dial that does nothing you can see. Hold the thought, because it turns out to be where the real cleverness lives later.

Now the words people use. You will constantly hear that a qubit is "0 and 1 at the same time". It is the wrong words. The arrow is set to **one** definite direction. "Both at once" suggests you could read out both answers, and you cannot: a look gives a single 0 or a single 1, never two. A row of qubits is not quietly storing every combination at once either. Drop the "at once" and you are already ahead of most of the headlines.

It is fair to ask how something can be definite and yet only give odds. Picture a coin not spinning in the air but resting on its edge: settled, doing something definite, yet not committed to heads or tails. That is a better image than a tumbling coin, because there is nothing frantic about a qubit in its tilt. But the coin smuggles in something untrue. A hidden coin secretly already has its answer; you just do not know it yet. A qubit genuinely does not: before you look, the answer is not already sitting there waiting to be uncovered. That difference sounds philosophical and is in fact the most practical thing in the subject. Keep it in your pocket.

<details class="qcheck">
<summary>Point the arrow so a look almost always gives 0, is that near the top or the bottom?</summary>

Near the top. The tilt alone sets the odds: aim it towards 0 (the top) and a look almost always comes back 0.

</details>

So a qubit is a direction you set, where the tilt sets the odds, sitting in a state that is not already decided, with a sideways dial we have parked. The question that catches everyone out: if you cannot read the direction, how do you get an answer out of it?

<p class="recap">Recap: a qubit is an arrow you tilt; the tilt sets the odds of 0 or 1. A parked sideways setting comes back later.</p>

## 4. How do you get an answer out?

This is the one genuinely hard idea, so let me slow down. The hard part is not your ability; it is that it cuts against an instinct we never notice we lean on: that looking is reading. The number on the scale was there before you glanced at it.

A qubit is not like that. The only thing you may do is **look**, and looking is not reading a value that was sitting there. It forces the qubit to commit: a plain 0 or 1, with odds set by the tilt. And here is the part most explanations skip, the one to keep if you keep nothing else: the instant it answers, the tilt is gone. Not hidden, gone. Your instinct is to look again, more carefully, to pin down the direction. It cannot work, because there is nothing left to look at. That one answer is all the qubit will ever tell you about how it was tilted.

<BlochLab measure />

Before you press, predict: set a tilt well up towards 0 and ask which answer is the safe bet. Then look once. You get a single 0 or 1, and the arrow snaps flat, spent.

Which raises the obvious question: if one look gives one answer and wipes the tilt, how could you learn the odds? You start over. Prepare a fresh qubit tilted exactly the same way, and look at that, and another. That is all "look 100x" is: a hundred separate qubits, each tilted identically and looked at once. Tally them and the split settles close to the odds, though not exactly, because a hundred looks is still a sample. The honest picture of measurement is never one dramatic snap; it is a tally that emerges after many shots.

One more thing, because it is easy to assume randomness lives everywhere. It does not. Everything you do to tilt and turn the arrow beforehand is completely predictable and, in principle, undoable. The roll of the dice enters at exactly one moment: the look.

So we leave with two facts. The tilt only ever sets odds, never the answer. And a look spends those odds on a single 0 or 1 and destroys the tilt. Hold tight to the second, because the obvious clever idea in the next section but one, just blur the qubit over every answer and read off the winner, dies precisely on it.

<details class="qcheck">
<summary>You looked and got 1. Try to recover the original tilt, what stops you?</summary>

Nothing is left to look at. The look destroyed the tilt, so there is no direction still sitting there to recover. To learn the odds you would prepare a fresh, identically tilted qubit and look again.

</details>

<p class="recap">Recap: looking forces a single 0 or 1 and destroys the tilt; you learn the odds only by preparing the same arrow afresh and looking many times.</p>

<p class="idea-step">Idea 3 of 3 &middot; the hidden lever</p>

## 5. The hidden lever: phase

Remember the setting I asked you to park. As well as tilting the arrow, you can turn it sideways, and that changed nothing about the odds when you looked. I called it pointless and promised it does the real work. This is where I make good, because it is the idea that takes you from "I get the pictures" to "I could follow what an algorithm is doing".

Its honest name is the **phase**. I have called it the sideways setting because that is what it is: a direction the arrow can face, at right angles to the up-down tilt. It is a real, physical setting, as adjustable as the tilt. The strange part is that it is invisible when you look straight on.

Let me show you what invisible means, precisely. Take an arrow tilted to a perfect fifty-fifty. Spin that sideways setting all the way round and watch the odds. They do not move. If you only ever looked straight on, you would swear you were turning a dial wired to nothing. That reaction is right, and it is a trap: the setting really is changing, you just cannot see it from where you stand.

<PhaseLever />

So spin it first, and watch the odds stay perfectly still. Then do the one thing that changes everything: turn to look at the same arrow from the side. Now spin the setting again. This time the odds sweep, smoothly and completely, from almost-always-0 to almost-always-1, and where they land is fixed entirely by the setting. No randomness in the sweep. You did not change the setting between the two goes; you changed how you looked.

Two myths die here. The first is that phase is cosmetic. It is the opposite: two arrows with identical odds head-on can have opposite futures the moment you look from the side. The second is that a setting you cannot see straight on must be doing nothing. "I cannot see it from here" and "it is not there" are different statements, and the gap between them is most of quantum computing. You never read the setting off directly, either; the only way you learn it is the way you just did, by changing how you look and watching how it steers the odds.

That deterministic sweep, the odds gliding from 0 to 1 as you turn the setting, *is* interference, in miniature. When the routes to an answer agree they reinforce and the odds pile up; when they disagree they cancel and the odds drain away. This is the lever the famous algorithms pull, every time.

<details class="aside-note">
<summary>Why the toy only moves the setting that is physically real</summary>

A fair question: the arrow lives in three dimensions, so are there settings I am not letting you touch? One, and leaving it out is deliberate. There is a way of "turning" the whole arrow that changes nothing measurable from any direction at all. That one is not a lever; it is a free choice of where you call zero, and physics does not care about it. The deep piece names the distinction properly (relative phase, the real one, versus global phase, the free one). Here I have only let you move the lever that does something, because a dial wired to nothing would teach a fiction.

</details>

<details class="qcheck">
<summary>The odds did not budge when you spun the setting, then they swept end to end. What changed, the setting or how you looked?</summary>

How you looked. The setting was real and working the whole time; looking straight on simply cannot see it, and looking from the side is what lets it steer the answer. That steering is interference in miniature.

</details>

## 6. So how could it ever help?

This is the part almost every popular explanation gets wrong, mine included the first time I tried to explain it to a friend.

The tempting story: the machine puts itself into a blur over every possible answer, checks them all in parallel, and hands you the winner. It is half right. A quantum computer genuinely can start from a blur over all the answers. It is the second half, "and then it reads off the right one", that does not happen, and the last section is why. Looking is destructive. Sit a machine in a blur over every answer, look, and you pull out one of them at random, no better than shutting your eyes and guessing. That is Scott Aaronson's point: if a plain look was all you were going to do, you could have skipped the quantum computer and picked an answer yourself. A blur on its own buys you nothing.

So the blur is not the trick. The trick is what you do to it before you look. A quantum computation reaches the same answer by several routes at once, and each route carries something like the crest or the trough of a wave. Where routes meet, they can line up and add, or sit out of step and flatten each other to nothing, the way noise-cancelling headphones play a sound shaped to cancel the noise. The whole craft is to arrange it so the routes to every *wrong* answer cancel, while the routes to the *right* answer reinforce. Do that, and a look returns the right answer overwhelmingly, not by luck and not by checking everything, but because the wrong answers have deleted themselves.

The toy below is that idea, stripped to its bones, and it is what a computer actually does with interference: not a fringe pattern of many bright spots, but a deliberate arrangement over the candidate answers. Six answers start as an equal blur, so a look gives one at random. Drag to arrange the interference: the contributions to the wrong answers fan out and cancel, the right one stays lined up and reinforces, until a look almost always returns it.

<Amplify />

It is worth not skating over how strange cancellation is. For two contributions to cancel, one must be able to push the other way: a crest against a trough, a plus against a minus. That is the quiet difference that does all the work. An ordinary computer only ever combines plain chances, and a chance is never less than nothing, so adding more only ever gives more. A quantum computer lets each route carry a *signed* contribution, a plus or a minus that becomes a plain chance only at the end, when you look and square it up. Signs can cancel; chances cannot. That is the lever an ordinary computer does not have.

(You will hear this dressed up as "computing in parallel universes". That is one way some physicists choose to talk, and others disagree just as firmly. It is a disputed interpretation, not the mechanism, and the cancelling works the same whether or not you find it appealing.)

Arranging that cancellation on purpose is hard, and it only pays off when a problem has the right structure: simulating molecules and materials, certain optimisation, and, more speculatively, parts of machine learning. For most tasks there is no cancellation to arrange and no advantage at all, and even on the promising ones we are early and narrow. No curing every disease tomorrow, no breaking all encryption next year. The honest version is smaller and, I think, far more interesting.

<details class="qcheck">
<summary>A machine blurs itself over every answer, then you look. Why is that no better than guessing?</summary>

Because a look pulls out one answer at random, and a blur on its own gives you no reason for it to be the right one. The craft is to make the wrong answers cancel first, so the right one is what is overwhelmingly left.

</details>

## A short word on entanglement

Entanglement gets all the attention, so it would be odd to skip it, but it sits a storey up and deserves a careful page rather than a crammed paragraph. The taste: put two qubits together and you can link them so the description that says how they behave is a description of the *pair*, which genuinely cannot be split into "what the first is doing" and, separately, "what the second is doing". Look and the first answers 0, and you instantly know the second will too, even though neither had a settled answer a moment before. That is not spooky action or parallel universes; nothing travels between them. The correlation was built into their shared description from the start and only shows up when you measure.

<aside class="gloss">A surprise worth flagging: examine one qubit of an entangled pair on its own and you find nothing in particular, just an even shrug. All the structure is in the relationship, none in the part, which is exactly why two separate arrows can never draw it.</aside>

One honest note, because it matters and it is close to my own work: it is tempting to treat entanglement as the special sauce, so more must mean more power. In the most careful benchmarks I have seen, often on classification tasks, stripping the entanglement out frequently does not hurt the results, and sometimes the plainer model does better. It is necessary for some of what quantum computers can do, but it is not a dial you turn up for free. The careful answer is usually more interesting than the loud one.

## Where to go next

That is the honest core, and it is genuinely the shape of the thing, not a simplified version with the hard parts removed: a qubit is a direction you set, looking forces an answer and destroys the rest, a hidden setting steers the odds once you look from the side, and the craft is arranging interference so the wrong answers cancel and the right one reinforces. If those clicked, you can follow a conversation about quantum computing without being sold a myth.

What I deliberately left in its box is the machinery: what the "direction" really is, the signed amounts that make cancellation possible, how you choreograph the interference on purpose, and the algorithms built from it (including where this goes for machine learning, the part nearest my own work).

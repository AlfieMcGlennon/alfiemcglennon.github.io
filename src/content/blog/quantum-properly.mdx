## What a quantum computer is actually for

Let me start by taking something off the table. A quantum computer is not a faster laptop. It will not make your email load quicker, your spreadsheets recalculate sooner, or your games run smoother. For very nearly everything you do on a computer all day, a quantum machine would be no better than a pocket calculator, and usually much worse. That is not a flaw in the technology. It is the whole point: it is a different kind of machine, built for a different kind of job.

I work in climate and machine learning, and quantum computing keeps turning up at the edge of my own research, so I have spent a good while getting to grips with it. This is the plain-English version I wish someone had handed me at the start. No equations, no hype, and none of the lines you have probably already heard. I have a companion piece that goes properly into the machinery, and this is the gentle way in to it.

So if it is not a faster laptop, what is it for? The honest answer is a surprisingly short list. A quantum computer is expected, one day, to be genuinely good at simulating nature itself, which means the behaviour of molecules and materials, the kind of thing chemists and materials scientists would love to predict without years in a lab. It may help with certain sorts of optimisation problems. And, more speculatively, with parts of machine learning. That is roughly the list. Outside it, there is no expected advantage at all. So the interesting question was never "how fast". It is "good at what", and the honest answer is "a few specific things, and we are still early".

I want to give you the honest shape of the whole thing before we go near any detail. If you read nothing else, read this box. The rest of the post is just these four lines, slowed right down.

<aside class="callout callout-key">

**The whole idea, in four lines**

1. A qubit is a *direction you set*, not a switch that is somehow both on and off.
2. You cannot read that direction. *Looking forces a plain 0 or 1, and destroys the rest.*
3. The trick is to arrange things so the *wrong answers cancel out* and the *right one reinforces*.
4. It is *early and narrow*. For most tasks, including ones in my own field, there is no clear advantage yet.

</aside>

If even that feels like a lot, do not worry. Every line gets its own section, with something to play with, and by the end it should all feel ordinary.

Now, the reason a post like this is worth writing at all. Quantum computing has picked up a small collection of catchy phrases that are repeated everywhere, including in places that ought to know better, and every one of them quietly points you in the wrong direction. So before we begin, I want to name them out loud. Whenever you see one of these in the wild, treat it as a flag that someone has reached for the easy phrasing rather than the true one.

<aside class="callout callout-refuse">

**Three lines we are refusing**

- *"It tries every answer at once."* The single most common one, and the one the whole post is built to correct. It feels like it must be the secret, and it is not.
- *"A qubit is 0 and 1 at the same time."* The wrong words for something real. It quietly suggests you could read out both answers, which you cannot.
- *"It uses parallel universes."* A fun, hotly disputed interpretation that some serious people argue for, but it is not the mechanism, and you do not need it to understand anything here.

</aside>

I am not just waving these away. Each one is half-pointing at something genuine, and the satisfying part is seeing exactly where each one goes wrong. That is most of what the rest of this page is for: not a list of facts to take on trust, but the small set of ideas that let you spot the dodgy phrasing yourself. The first myth, "tries every answer at once", is the big one, and we will take it apart properly once you have the two tools you need for it. By the end you should not just know it is wrong. You should be able to say why, in a sentence, to a friend.

## Why is everyone suddenly talking about it?

Three real things happened at roughly the same time, and it is worth being honest about each one, because each comes with a caveat that the headlines tend to drop. Keep both halves together and the picture stops feeling like hype and starts feeling like what it is: a young field moving in the right direction, with a long way still to go.

The first is a genuine milestone. The hardest problem in building one of these machines is that the parts are fragile: they pick up errors constantly, and the obvious fix, adding more parts to check the work, has historically just added more things that can go wrong. The goal since the 1990s has been a turning point where adding more error-prone parts makes the encoded result *cleaner* rather than messier. In December 2024, Google's Willow chip crossed that line for the first time, below what the field calls threshold. That is real, and it is the thing error correction has to do to ever scale. The caveat, and Google says this themselves, is that the remaining gap is enormous: roughly one error in a thousand today, against the one in a trillion a genuinely useful machine would need. A marker on a long road, not the end of it. And notice there is no date attached, by them or by me.

<aside class="gloss">Willow "below threshold", December 2024: Google Quantum AI, <em>Nature</em> 638, 920 (2024), doi:10.1038/s41586-024-08449-y. The one-in-a-thousand to one-in-a-trillion framing is Google's own.</aside>

The second is a steady trend rather than a single event. The reliability of the basic two-qubit operation, the move that lets two qubits actually interact, has climbed over years: from coin-flip unreliable, to roughly nine in ten, to ninety-nine in a hundred, to better than 99.9% in the best labs (the three-nines mark was first crossed by Ballance and Lucas in Oxford in 2016). The caveat here is gentler but still matters: this is a trend line, not a finish line. A line going the right way tells you the engineering is working. It does not tell you when, and I am not going to pretend it does.

<aside class="gloss">Two-qubit gate fidelity climbing roughly 50 to 90 to 99 to 99.9%; the three-nines mark first crossed by Ballance and Lucas (Oxford), Phys. Rev. Lett. (2016).</aside>

The third is a wall of money. Investment in quantum start-ups reached around 12.6 billion dollars in 2025, roughly six times the year before, with about nine in every ten of those dollars going into quantum computing specifically. This is the one I would ask you to be most careful with, because it is the one most easily misread. Money measures belief, not capability. A wave of funding tells you a lot of serious people are betting it will work; it tells you nothing about whether it works yet. Capability is measured in the lab, in errors and fidelities, not on a balance sheet.

<aside class="gloss">About 12.6 billion USD into quantum start-ups in 2025, roughly 6.3 times the prior year, around 90% into quantum computing: McKinsey Quantum Technology Monitor (2026).</aside>

So the headlines make sense, and the excitement is not baseless. But put the three legs together honestly and you land somewhere calmer than the noise. We are still in the small, noisy era. The machines that exist today are nowhere near the genuinely useful applications, which most likely need millions of well-behaved qubits rather than the hundreds of shaky ones we have. And here is the part worth carrying with you: the loudest claims have tended to be the ones to distrust. Several of the loudest headline claims of the past couple of years drew serious public pushback from other scientists. That is not cynicism. It is the field correcting itself in public, which is exactly what you want to see. The honest read is that something real is happening, slowly, and the way to follow it is to treat every single headline as a question, not an answer.

## What is a qubit?

A normal bit is a switch. It is either 0 or 1, on or off, and there is nothing in between. A qubit is the thing people reach for the wrong words to describe, so let me give you the picture that finally made it sit still for me: a qubit is an **arrow you can tilt**.

Point the arrow straight up and it means 0. Point it straight down and it means 1. So far that is just a switch drawn vertically. The interesting part is everything in between. The arrow can rest at any angle, and where you aim it sets the **odds** of getting 0 or 1 when you eventually look. Tilt it near the top and a look will almost always give you 0. Tilt it near the bottom and almost always 1. Leave it pointing sideways, halfway between, and it is roughly a coin toss. That is the whole idea: a qubit is a direction you have chosen, and the tilt sets the odds.

<BlochLab />

Have a play. Tilt the arrow and watch how the odds shift as it swings from top to bottom. Notice there is no special "both" position. Every angle is just one definite direction with its own particular odds, and the only thing that changes as you tilt is how those odds lean.

There is one more thing you can do to the arrow, and I am going to mention it now and then deliberately leave it alone. As well as tilting up or down, you can swivel the arrow to face different sideways directions, like turning to point north or east while keeping the same height. Here is the curious bit: that sideways setting changes nothing about the odds when you look once. Tilt fixed, you can swivel it all the way round and a single look behaves exactly the same. So for now it looks pointless, a dial you can turn that does nothing you can see. If "a setting you can change but never notice" sounds like it should not matter, that is the right instinct for the moment. Hold the thought anyway, because that quiet sideways dial turns out to be where the real cleverness lives later on. We are parking it on purpose, not forgetting it.

Now for the words people usually use, because they are worth clearing up while the picture is fresh. You will constantly hear that a qubit is "0 and 1 at the same time". It is a tempting line and it is the wrong words. The arrow is set to **one** definite direction. "Both at once" quietly suggests you could somehow read out both answers, and you cannot: when you look, you get a single 0 or a single 1, never two. The qubit is not secretly holding both answers in reserve, and a row of qubits is not quietly storing every possible combination at once either. There is no magical hidden warehouse of answers in there. Where the arrow points is real, specific, and singular. It is also not "in two places at once", which is the same muddle wearing a different coat. One arrow, one direction. Drop the "at once" and you are already ahead of most of the headlines.

It is fair to ask how something can be definite and yet only give you odds. The honest comparison is a coin, and it is worth getting it exactly right because the usual version of it misleads. Picture a coin not spinning in the air but resting still on its edge. It is settled, it is doing something definite, and yet it has not committed to heads or tails. That is a far better image for a qubit than a coin tumbling through the air, because there is nothing frantic or random about a qubit sitting in its tilt. It is just resting at an angle.

But I have to break my own analogy, gently, because the coin smuggles in something untrue. A spinning coin, or even a coin hidden under your hand, secretly already has its answer. It landed heads or tails; you simply do not know yet. The "odds" there are just your ignorance of a fact that already exists. A qubit is genuinely not like that. Before you look, the tilted arrow does not secretly already know whether it will say 0 or 1. The answer is not sitting there waiting to be uncovered. That difference sounds almost philosophical, but it is the most practical thing in the whole subject, and it is exactly what makes the later sections possible. When we get to how a quantum computer could ever help, that gap, between "the answer was already there, hidden" and "the answer is not already sitting there at all, waiting to be read", is the whole game. So keep "definite, but not a fact already waiting to be read" in your pocket. We will come back for it.

That is genuinely most of what a qubit is. A direction you set, where the tilt sets the odds, sitting in a definite state that is not already settled in advance, with a quiet sideways dial we have left parked for now. The natural next question is the one that catches everyone out: if you cannot read the direction, how on earth do you get an answer out of it?

## How do you get an answer out?

Everything up to here has been friendly. This next idea is the one genuinely hard part of the whole post, the thing that trips up almost everyone the first time, so let me slow right down. The hard part is not your ability, it is that this idea cuts against an instinct so deep we never notice we are leaning on it. Once it lands, the rest of quantum computing stops feeling like magic and starts feeling like engineering.

Here is the instinct. With anything ordinary, looking is reading. The number on the scale was already there before you glanced at it; your looking just told you what it was. So when I say a qubit is an arrow tilted to some direction, you naturally expect that you could look and find out which way it points, the way you would read a dial.

You cannot. The only thing you are allowed to do is **look**, and looking is not reading a value that was sitting there waiting. Looking forces the qubit to commit. It answers with a plain 0 or a plain 1, nothing in between, and which one you get is down to the odds the tilt was holding. Tilted near the top, you will almost certainly get 0. Tilted near the bottom, almost certainly 1. Tilted sideways, it is close to a coin toss. The arrow set the odds; the look spends them on a single answer.

And here is the part most explanations quietly skip, the one I would beg you to keep if you keep nothing else from this section. The instant the qubit answers, the tilt is gone. Not hidden, not used up for now, gone. Your honest instinct is to look again, more carefully this time, to pin down the direction the first look was too crude to catch. It is exactly the right instinct and it cannot work, because there is no longer anything there to look at. That one answer is the whole of what the qubit will ever tell you about how it was tilted.

<BlochLab measure />

So before you press the button, predict. Set a tilt well up towards 0 and ask yourself which answer is the safe bet. Then look once. You get a single 0 or 1, and the arrow snaps flat to whatever it said, spent. One look, one answer, no second chances.

Which raises the obvious question. If a single look gives one answer and wipes the tilt, how could anyone ever learn the odds the tilt was holding? You cannot squeeze them out of one qubit. What you do instead is start over. Prepare a fresh qubit, tilted exactly the same way, and look at that. Then another, and another. That is all "look 100x" is doing: a hundred separate qubits, each tilted identically and each looked at once, because every look spends its own qubit. Tally the results and the split settles close to the odds the tilt was holding. Close, not exact, because a hundred looks is still only a sample, the same way a hundred coin tosses rarely land on a perfect fifty-fifty. Run it again and the numbers wobble a little. The honest picture of measurement is never one dramatic snap; it is a tally that emerges only after many shots.

While we are being careful, one more thing worth nailing down, because it is easy to assume the randomness lives everywhere. It does not. Everything you do to tilt and turn the arrow beforehand is completely predictable and, in principle, undoable; nudge it the same way twice and it lands in the same place twice. The roll of the dice enters at exactly one moment, the look. That clean split, predictable handling and randomness only on measurement, is worth carrying with you.

So we leave this section holding two facts, and they are the load-bearing pair for what comes next. One: the tilt only ever sets odds, never the answer itself. Two: a look spends those odds on a single 0 or 1 and destroys the tilt for good. Hold especially tight to that second one, because the obvious clever idea in the next section, just put the qubit into a blur over every possible answer and read off the winner, dies precisely on it. You do not get to read off anything. You get one answer, the tilt is gone, and that is why the real trick has to be something far cleverer than looking.

## The hidden lever (the sideways setting, made visible)

Remember the setting I asked you to park. When we talked about the arrow, I mentioned that as well as tilting it up or down, you can turn it to face different sideways directions, and that this sideways setting changed nothing about the odds when you looked. I called it pointless and promised it does the real work. This is where I make good on that, because it turns out to be the most important idea in the whole post, and it is the one that takes you from "I get the pictures" to "I could follow what an algorithm is actually doing".

Here is the honest name for it. That sideways setting is the **phase**. I have been calling it the sideways setting because that is exactly what it is, a direction the arrow can face that is at right angles to the up-down tilt, and I would rather you carried the picture than the word. The word is just a label. The thing the label points at is a real, physical setting on the qubit, every bit as adjustable as the tilt. The strange part, the part that makes it feel pointless at first, is that it is invisible when you look straight on.

Let me show you what "invisible" means, because it is precise, not vague. Take an arrow tilted to a perfect fifty-fifty, sideways, halfway between 0 and 1. Now spin that sideways setting all the way round, through every value it can take. Watch the odds the whole time. They do not move. Not a little, not at all. Fifty-fifty stays fifty-fifty no matter where you point it sideways. If you only ever looked straight on, you would swear nothing was happening, that you were turning a dial wired to nothing. That reaction is correct, and it is also a trap. The setting really is changing. You just cannot see the change from where you are standing.

<PhaseLever />

So spin it first. Watch the odds sit perfectly still while the setting sweeps round, and let that be properly strange for a moment. Then do the one thing that changes everything: turn to look at the same arrow from the side.

Now spin the setting again. This time the odds are not still at all. They sweep, smoothly and completely, from almost-always-0 all the way to almost-always-1 and back, and where they land is fixed entirely by where you put the setting. No randomness in the sweep itself. The same dial that did nothing a moment ago now steers the answer from end to end, deterministically, and you can watch it do it.

Sit with what just happened, because it is genuinely surprising and it is meant to be. You did not change the setting between the two goes. You changed how you looked. The sideways setting was real and doing its work the entire time, in both cases. Looking straight on simply cannot see it. Looking from the side can, and once you can see it, it is not subtle at all, it is the whole show.

A couple of myths get quietly killed here, and it is worth naming them so they do not creep back. The first is that phase is cosmetic, a bookkeeping detail with no consequences. It is the opposite of cosmetic. Two arrows with identical odds head-on, indistinguishable if that is all you ever check, can have opposite futures the instant you look from the side. The second is the idea that a setting you cannot see straight on must be doing nothing. It is doing plenty. It is doing the most important thing in the room. "I cannot see it from here" and "it is not there" are completely different statements, and the gap between them is most of quantum computing.

One more thing to head off, because it is the natural thing to want. You might expect that "setting the phase" should show you a number, the way tilting the arrow let you read the odds. It never does. The setting itself is never something you read out directly. The only way you ever learn it is the way you just did: change how you look, and watch how it steers the odds afterwards. The setting only ever speaks through its effect on what you finally see.

And that deterministic sweep, the odds gliding from 0 to 1 as you turn the setting, is interference. Not a metaphor for it, the thing itself, in miniature. When the routes to an answer agree, they reinforce and the odds pile up at that answer; when they disagree, they cancel and the odds drain away. The sweep you are watching is exactly that, agreement and cancellation traded smoothly back and forth by one hidden setting. This is the hinge. The cancelling-and-reinforcing I described earlier, the whole reason a quantum computer can ever help, is built out of this one move repeated and arranged on purpose. When the companion piece gets to the famous algorithms, this is the lever they are pulling, every time.

## So how could it ever help?

This is the part almost every popular explanation gets wrong, mine included the first time I tried to explain it to a friend. So let me slow right down here, because if one idea from this whole page is worth carrying away, it is this one.

The tempting story goes like this: the machine quietly puts itself into a blur over every possible answer at once, checks them all in parallel, and hands you the winner. It is the line you have almost certainly heard, and it is worth saying plainly that it is half right. A quantum computer genuinely can start from a blur over all the answers. That part is real. It is the second half, the "and then it reads off the right one", that quietly does not happen.

Here is why, and this is where the last section pays off. Remember that looking is destructive: the instant you look, you get one plain answer and the rest is gone for good. So picture the machine sitting in that blur over every answer, and picture yourself looking. You would just pull out one of those answers at random, no better than if you had shut your eyes and guessed. As Scott Aaronson puts it, you could have saved yourself a great deal of money. A blur on its own buys you nothing. Parallelism alone is worthless.

### The real move: cancel the wrong answers

So a blur is not the trick. The trick is what you do to that blur before you look.

A quantum computation can arrive at the same answer by several different routes at the same time, and here is the crucial thing those routes carry that an ordinary computer's do not. Each route comes with something like the crest or the trough of a wave. When two routes meet, their contributions can line up and add together, or they can sit out of step and flatten each other to nothing, the way two sound waves can meet and cancel into silence. That is exactly how noise-cancelling headphones work: they play a sound shaped to cancel the noise, so the two add up to quiet.

The whole craft of quantum computing is to set things up so that the routes leading to every wrong answer arrive out of step and cancel each other out, while the routes leading to the right answer line up and reinforce. Do that, and when you finally look, the right answer is overwhelmingly the one you get. Not by luck, and not by checking everything. By arranging for the wrong answers to delete themselves first.

### The honest bit that makes it non-magical

It is easy to nod along to "they cancel" without noticing how strange that is, so let me not skate over it. For two contributions to cancel, one of them has to be able to push the other way. They carry a sign. One can be a crest where the other is a trough, a plus against a minus, and that is the only reason adding two real things together can ever leave you with less than you started with, or with nothing at all.

This is the quiet difference that does all the work. An ordinary computer only ever combines plain chances, and a chance is never less than nothing: add more of them and you only ever get more, nothing can ever subtract. A quantum computer lets each route carry a signed contribution instead, a plus or a minus that only turns into a plain chance at the very end, when you look and square it up. Because those contributions carry a sign, they can push against each other and cancel along the way. That is the lever an ordinary computer simply does not have. Once you see it, the magic drains out of the word interference and you are left with something almost mechanical: a careful arrangement of pluses and minuses so the wrong answers add to zero.

(You will sometimes hear this dressed up as the machine "computing in parallel universes". That is one way some physicists choose to talk about it, and others disagree just as firmly. It is a disputed flavour, an interpretation, not the mechanism. The cancelling is the mechanism, and it works exactly the same whether or not you find the parallel-universes story appealing.)

### So what is it actually good for?

Arranging that cancellation on purpose is genuinely hard, and it only pays off when a problem has the right kind of structure to exploit, something you can set up so the wrong answers really do line up to cancel. That turns out to be a short list: simulating quantum systems like molecules and materials, certain kinds of optimisation, and, more speculatively, parts of machine learning. For most of what a computer does all day, there is no clever cancellation to arrange and so no advantage at all. And even on the promising problems we are early and narrow: the machines are small and noisy, and a clear, decisive win over an ordinary computer is something the field is still working towards, not something it can point to today. No curing every disease tomorrow, no breaking all the world's encryption next year. The honest version is smaller and, I think, far more interesting than the hype.

## A gentle word on entanglement

Entanglement is the word that gets all the attention, so it would be odd to leave it out entirely. But it sits one storey up from the ideas we have been building, and I want to give you a clear taste of it rather than pretend a single paragraph can do it justice. The honest version, with pictures that actually hold up, is in the companion piece. Here is the taste.

Everything so far has been about one qubit: one arrow, one tilt, one sideways setting. Now put two qubits together. Your first instinct is the sensible one: two arrows, each with its own tilt, sitting side by side. For a lot of two-qubit states that picture is exactly right, and you lose nothing by thinking of them as a pair of separate arrows.

Entanglement is what happens when that picture quietly stops working. You can prepare two qubits so that they are linked: the description that tells you how they will behave is a description of the *pair*, all at once, and it genuinely cannot be split back into "this is what the first one is doing, and separately, this is what the second one is doing". The information lives in the joint description, in how the two are correlated, not in either qubit on its own.

A flat-footed way to say it: the rules now live in a little table of the *combinations*. For two entangled qubits you might have arranged things so that "both come out 0" and "both come out 1" are the only outcomes with any weight, and "one of each" cancels away to nothing, the same arranging-so-things-cancel trick from the last section, just applied to pairs. So the moment you look and the first qubit answers 0, you already know the second will answer 0 as well, even though neither qubit had a settled answer of its own a moment before. The correlation was real and waiting in the joint table; the individual answers were not sitting there in advance.

And that is the whole mechanism, so it is worth saying plainly what it is *not*. It is not spooky action at a distance, with one qubit reaching across the room to shove the other. It is not the two qubits living in separate parallel universes. Nothing travels between them when you look. What you are seeing is a correlation that was built into their shared description from the start, finally showing up when you measure. "Spooky" is the word people reach for when the joint description is left out; once it is in, the spookiness goes with it.

Here is the part I most want you to take away, because it matters for honesty later. It is tempting to read all this as "entanglement is the special sauce, so more of it must mean more power". That is not how it has turned out. In the most careful benchmarks we have, often on classification tasks, stripping the entanglement out frequently does not hurt the results, and sometimes the plainer model does better. Entanglement is real and it is necessary for some of the things quantum computers can do, but it is not a dial where turning it up automatically buys you more. That honesty is the same honesty I try to carry into my own corner of this field, where the careful answer is usually more interesting than the loud one.

<aside class="gloss">A surprise worth flagging: take one qubit out of an entangled pair and examine it completely on its own, and you find nothing in particular, no tilt, no settled direction, just an even shrug. All the structure is in the relationship, none of it in the part. That is exactly why two separate arrows can never capture entanglement, and why the honest picture lives in the deep piece.</aside>

So that is the taste, and I am leaving it as a taste on purpose. There is a lovely picture for what an entangled pair actually looks like, but it belongs with the proper treatment next door, not crammed in here.

## Want the actual machinery?

If you have read this far, you are further in than you might think. Here is the whole post in four sentences. A qubit is a direction you set, an arrow you can tilt. You cannot read that direction; looking forces a plain 0 or 1, with odds set by the tilt, and the instant it answers the tilt is gone. There is a sideways setting that does nothing when you look straight on, but turn to look from the side and that same hidden setting steers the answer on purpose, which is interference in miniature. And the whole craft is choreographing that interference so the routes to the wrong answers cancel and the routes to the right answer reinforce, which is a very different thing from trying everything at once.

That really is the honest core. Not a simplified version of the truth with the hard parts quietly removed, but the actual shape of how a quantum computer earns its keep, told without the maths. If those four ideas clicked, you are not waiting at the door of the subject; you are standing inside the first room of it, and you can follow a conversation about quantum computing without being sold a myth.

So why is there more? Because I made a deliberate choice on this page to keep one honest idea per section and to leave the gears in their box. Here is exactly what I parked, so you can see the omissions are a plan rather than gaps:

- **What the "direction" really is.** The tilt and that sideways setting are two numbers on a sphere, and there is a precise reason the sideways one hides from a straight-on look and reappears from the side. The arrow was the honest picture; the deep piece shows you the actual object.
- **The amounts that can go negative.** Cancellation has to come from somewhere. The routes carry amounts that can point the other way and subtract, and an ordinary computer is, in a real sense, a quantum one stuck using only positive amounts. That single difference is the whole game, and it deserves more than a sentence.
- **How you choreograph the interference on purpose.** Lining up the right answer and flattening the wrong ones does not happen by luck. There is a craft to it, and the deep piece builds it up step by step rather than asserting it.
- **The algorithms that made everyone pay attention.** The famous ones, the search and the factoring, are just this choreography aimed at a specific target. Once the mechanism is in place they stop being magic and start being method.
- **And where this goes for machine learning.** This is the part nearest my own work: how a single qubit, fed the same data again and again, turns into a surprisingly capable little model, and the honest reckoning of whether that ever beats a classical one. (The short version, which I will not soften: for anything in weather and climate, no published result shows a clear advantage yet.)

None of that is a correction of what you have just read. It is the next layer on the same foundation.

If you want it, the next step is one piece, and it is the start of a short series: **[Quantum Computing from First Principles](/writing/quantum-computing-explainer/)**. Think of it less as a wall to climb and more as a sandbox you graduate into, with the same arrows and the same honesty, room to actually turn the gears yourself. This page was the gentle front door. That one is where you go properly in.

## The resources that actually helped me

If you take one onward step from here, make it this one. The companion piece, [Quantum Computing from First Principles](/writing/quantum-computing-explainer/), is where everything we left out gets unpacked properly: what the "direction" really is, how you choreograph the cancellation on purpose, what happens when qubits are linked, and the famous algorithms. If the arrow and the cancelling clicked, you are ready for it.

Everything else below is a short shelf to dip into, roughly in the order I would hand it to a friend. It taught me far more than I could fit on one page, and pointing you to it is half the reason I wrote this. None of it is required reading, and it is deliberately tight: a couple of videos and a couple of reads, each with a one-line note on what it is good for.

**Watch**

- [Quantum Computing in 5 Levels of Difficulty](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OWJCfOvochA) (WIRED, with Talia Gershon of IBM). Good for your very first five minutes: a real researcher, an honest "not yet 0 or 1" prop, and the difficulty turned up one level at a time so you can stop wherever it stops being fun.
- [What makes quantum computers SO powerful?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-UrdExQW0cs) (Veritasium). Good for seeing the real mechanism: it centres interference, cancelling the wrong answers, rather than "trying everything at once".
- [But what is quantum computing? (Grover's Algorithm)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RQWpF2Gb-gU) (3Blue1Brown). Good for when you want the honest full picture: it opens by clearing up the misconceptions, then builds the interference idea visually. A notch more advanced, and worth it.
- [Don't fall for quantum hype](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b-aGIvUomTA) (Sabine Hossenfelder). Good as a reality check if the headlines have been getting to you, without dismissing the science underneath.

**Read**

- [Why Is Quantum Computing So Hard to Explain?](https://www.quantamagazine.org/why-is-quantum-computing-so-hard-to-explain-20210608/) (Scott Aaronson, Quanta). The one to read if you read only one: why "0 and 1 at once" and "tries everything at once" mislead, and what is actually going on instead.
- [What is Superposition?](https://www.quera.com/glossary/superposition) (QuEra). Good for a sixty-second honest definition that refuses the "0 and 1 at once" line.
- [Quantum Computing for the Very Curious](https://quantum.country/qcvc) (Nielsen and Matuschak). Good for going deeper at your own pace: a heavier, rewarding read that treats a qubit as an arrow and never reaches for the myth.
- [Basic Quantum Computing: Introduction](https://medium.com/@charlie.thomas_94667/basic-quantum-computing-introduction-f83dcfbd0e40) (Charlie Thomas). Good for a warm, from-scratch series to work through slowly, honest that the hard part is the order you learn things in, not your ability.

One to watch with a caveat: [Quantum Computers Explained](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JhHMJCUmq28) (Kurzgesagt) is the beautiful video you have probably already seen. It is genuinely lovely to look at, but it leans on the "calculate all states at once" line this whole post has been correcting. Worth watching as the framing to un-learn, not as the explainer to learn it from.
